The acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) compares recent training load to the athlete's recent baseline. AthleteIQ uses a 7-day acute sum divided by the 28-day chronic load expressed as an average week (28-day sum divided by 4). Gabbett's 2016 British Journal of Sports Medicine paper popularized this ratio and a widely quoted "sweet spot" of about 0.8–1.3, with values above 1.5 described as a danger zone for injury.

Those cutoffs are heuristics, not diagnostic thresholds. Later analyses (notably Impellizzeri and colleagues) argued that ACWR couples the numerator and denominator — acute load is part of the chronic window — which can create spurious injury associations. The original high-certainty claims around a 1.5 "danger" line have been walked back in the same literature; Section 18.2 of the AthleteIQ spec requires this controversy to be shown to coaches rather than hidden.

AthleteIQ still computes ACWR because a sharp week-to-week jump is a useful flag for the coach, but it is one input among several (recovery trend, persistent fatigue, Fitness–Fatigue). A high ACWR is not proof that an athlete will get injured, and a "sweet spot" reading is not proof they are safe. Treat the number as a conversation starter, not as a verdict.
